\chapter{Theoretical Foundations and Literature Review}\label{ch:review}\label{ch:background}\label{ch:litreview}

\section{Introduction to the Review}\label{sec:lr-intro}

A literature review situates a piece of research in its scientific field: it establishes what is already known, how it was established and with what limits, so that the contribution of the work can be stated precisely and defended. For this thesis it is all the more necessary as the subject lies at the crossing of three fields that rarely meet: the epidemiology of chronic diseases in sub-Saharan Africa, the clinical evaluation of telemonitoring, and the engineering of connected and predictive systems. The general context is that of the Introduction: hypertension and type~2 diabetes are frequent and often associated in Cameroon, their care fails mainly at follow-up, specialists are few, records are fragmented and connectivity is intermittent, but mobile telephony is widespread.

The review is built on nineteen core sources, eighteen peer-reviewed studies and the global report of the World Health Organization (WHO) on hypertension, each checked against PubMed, Crossref or the publisher's record; \cref{app:sources} summarises each of them. Two further studies, the TELEMED-CAM evaluation \parencite{kingue2013} and a home monitoring system \parencite{chatrati2022}, complete them, together with the guidelines of the WHO \parencite{who2021guideline,whoHEARTS} and of the European Society of Hypertension (ESH) \parencite{mancia2023}, from which the blood pressure thresholds are taken. Two architecture references \parencite{mucchi2021,yazdani2018} are used for the system design.

The chapter is organised as the school plan requires. \Cref{sec:concepts} defines the key concepts and the models that structure the reasoning. \Cref{sec:burden,sec:lr-regional,sec:evidence,sec:lr-iot,sec:lr-ml,sec:lr-privacy} analyse previous work in six themes, from the problem to the response (\cref{tab:lr-overview}); within each theme the studies are presented in chronological order, each study once, with its method, its main results and its limits. \Cref{sec:comparison} compares the approaches critically, \cref{sec:lit-gaps} identifies the limits of the existing literature, \cref{sec:research-questions} links them to the present research, and \cref{sec:lr-synthesis} summarises the review and prepares the transition to the methodology.

\subsection{Models structuring the work}\label{sec:models}

Four models structure the reasoning of the thesis (\cref{fig:concept-framework}).

\paragraph{The care cascade.} The WHO describes hypertension care as a cascade in which patients are lost at each stage \parencite{who2023hypertension}. The model locates where an intervention acts: screening acts on diagnosis, whereas longitudinal follow-up acts on treatment and control, where most diagnosed patients in Africa are lost (\cref{sec:cascade}).

\paragraph{The measurement model.} A reading is the patient's usual pressure plus a variable error, and repeated readings reduce the error in proportion to their number. This simple model justifies home self-measurement and the use of series rather than isolated values (\cref{sec:variability}).

\paragraph{The layered telemonitoring architecture.} Remote monitoring systems are described as a chain from acquisition to processing and visualisation \parencite{tai2024}, distributed over body, environment, edge and cloud layers \parencite{mucchi2021}. The model structures the system of \cref{sec:system}.

\paragraph{Supervised learning for clinical prediction.} A model maps features computed from a past window to a future clinical state. The framework fixes what a valid evaluation requires: a label outside the input window, proper measures suited to rare events, and a comparison with the guideline rule (\cref{sec:principle}). Behaviour change techniques, coded with the taxonomy used by \textcite{zhou2024}, complete it on the patient side.

\section{Burden of Hypertension and Diabetes}\label{sec:burden}\label{sec:hta-burden}

\subsection{The global burden of hypertension}

The WHO global report on hypertension \parencite{who2023hypertension} is the reference framing of the problem. For population surveillance it defines hypertension as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) $\geq 140$~mmHg, a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) $\geq 90$~mmHg, or the use of antihypertensive medication, in adults aged 30--79 years; this threshold is used throughout this thesis for epidemiological figures. The ESH uses the same threshold and divides hypertension into three grades \parencite{mancia2023}, which are used later to define the risk levels; some publications use the lower 130/80~mmHg threshold of the American guidelines, which explains part of the discrepancies between them.

The report establishes that the number of hypertensive adults rose from 650 million in 1990 to 1.3 billion in 2019, while the age-standardised prevalence barely changed, from 32\% to 33\%: population growth and ageing, not a rise in individual risk, drive the increase. Elevated systolic pressure is the leading risk factor for premature death among the 87 factors compared, ahead of smoking and hyperglycaemia, with 10.8 million deaths and 235 million disability-adjusted life years each year. Progress towards the global target is insufficient: the prevalence of uncontrolled hypertension fell only from 29\% in 2010 to 26\% in 2019, and no WHO region is on track. The report notes, however, that the target would be met if about half of the adults already diagnosed were effectively treated. Among the recommended responses, it endorses team-based care, community and home blood pressure measurement, and telemonitoring. As an institutional synthesis, the report relies on modelled estimates and does not address patient-level monitoring technology; it is used here for the global framing and for its normative positions, not as primary evidence.

\subsection{The failure of the care cascade}\label{sec:cascade}

The WHO structures care as a cascade of nested stages measured on the same denominator, all hypertensive adults (\cref{tab:cascade}). Two observations follow. The European Region has a higher prevalence than the African Region but a control rate more than twice as high: the African deficit is organisational rather than epidemiological. And the conditional losses are spread along the whole chain: in the African Region, 57\% of hypertensive adults are undiagnosed, 37\% of the diagnosed are untreated and 56\% of the treated are not controlled. Among patients already diagnosed, 72\% (31 of 43 points) are not controlled: they are known to the health system and lost during follow-up. Screening acts only on the first link of the chain; longitudinal follow-up acts on the second and third.

\subsection{The hypertension--diabetes comorbidity}\label{sec:comorbidity}

The association between hypertension and type~2 diabetes rests on shared risk factors---physical inactivity, unbalanced diet and obesity---and on a direct mechanism: diabetes stiffens small vessels, which raises peripheral resistance and blood pressure, and hypertension accelerates the vascular damage of diabetes.

\Textcite{hinneh2023} produced the first systematic quantification of this comorbidity in Africa. Their meta-analysis, conducted according to PRISMA on PubMed, EMBASE and HINARI up to March 2023, pooled 41 cross-sectional studies totalling 144\,433 participants: 58.1\% of people living with diabetes in Africa are also hypertensive (95\% CI 52.0--63.2), and 60.8\% of those with type~2 diabetes. The risk rises with age, excess weight, the number of years lived with diabetes, urban residence and male sex, and patients with both conditions paradoxically have lower chances of receiving appropriate care before complications occur. The sub-regional estimates place Central Africa highest (\cref{tab:subregional,fig:forest}).

\paragraph{Reliability of the Central African estimate.}\label{sec:central-estimates} The value of 77.6\% must be read with its interval. It rests on two small Cameroonian hospital series (109 and 210 patients), while three other Central African studies listed in the article (1\,832 patients, prevalences of 35.0--50.4\%) do not enter the subgroup. Heterogeneity is extreme ($I^2 \approx 98\%$), the included studies are mostly hospital-based with varying diagnostic thresholds, and the restriction to English-language publications under-represents francophone Central Africa, as the authors acknowledge. The figure also measures hypertension among people with diabetes, not the reverse. It cannot carry the case for this work on its own. That case rests on the continental estimate of 58.1\%, on the mechanism, and on the position of the WHO \parencite{who2023hypertension}: uncontrolled hypertension is among the risk factors most strongly associated with premature death in people with diabetes; Member States have adopted a target of 80\% of people with diagnosed diabetes having good blood pressure control; and the HEARTS package developed for hypertension is explicitly applicable to diabetes and other long-term conditions.

\subsection{Comparability of the estimates}\label{sec:comparability}

The prevalences quoted in this chapter are not directly comparable. They differ in reference population (general population, people with diabetes or hospital attendees), in diagnostic threshold (140/90 or 130/80~mmHg), in age structure, in the number of measurement visits---a single-visit measurement overestimates prevalence---and in recruitment, since opportunistic screening and self-selection produce samples that differ from probability sampling. None of the available sources is nationally representative for Cameroon. The nature of each estimate is therefore stated where it is used.

\subsection{Why one reading is not enough}\label{sec:variability}\label{sec:dx-requires}\label{sec:followup-requires}

The WHO diagnostic protocol is longitudinal by construction \parencite{who2023hypertension}. Blood pressure is measured twice at the first encounter, under standard conditions (seated, back supported, appropriate cuff), and the decision depends on the level observed (\cref{tab:protocol}); hypertension is diagnosed only if the threshold is exceeded at two visits. A care model that cannot guarantee the second visit does not produce imperfect diagnoses: it produces none.

\centering\small

\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{6.2cm} X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Pressure on both readings of the day} & \textbf{Recommended action} \\
\midrule
SBP $\geq$ 160 or DBP $\geq$ 100~mmHg & start treatment the same day \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The reason is the variability of blood pressure. A reading of patient $i$ at time $t$ can be written $b_{i,t} = \mu_i + \varepsilon_{i,t}$, where $\mu_i$ is the patient's usual pressure, the clinically relevant quantity, and $\varepsilon_{i,t}$ gathers circadian variation, activity, stress, the measurement setting and instrument error. With $n$ readings far enough apart for their errors to be independent,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 / n .
\end{equation}
A consultation provides two to four readings a year; home follow-up provides several dozen (\cref{fig:variability}), which estimate $\mu_i$ far more precisely and also estimate the patient's own variability $\sigma_\varepsilon$. This is the central argument for home measurement.

Follow-up must moreover be frequent. Recommendation~7 of the WHO guideline on the pharmacological treatment of hypertension sets monthly follow-up after any treatment change until the target is reached, then every three to six months \parencite{who2021guideline}, and the HEARTS treatment-protocol module adds that the return interval should usually be four weeks or less during titration \parencite{whoHEARTS}. With 56\% of treated patients uncontrolled in the African Region, the number of contacts required per patient and per year is
\begin{equation}
  \bar n = 0.56 \times 12 + 0.44 \times n_c \approx 7.6 \text{ to } 8.5, \qquad n_c \in [2,4],
\end{equation}
where $n_c$ is the number of yearly contacts of a controlled patient. An episodic model that delivers one or two contacts a year delivers between an eighth and a quarter of what the guideline requires. This cumulative gap, more than the length of each consultation, is the structural deficit that telemonitoring must fill. It also explains therapeutic inertia: intensifying treatment requires a history showing that the target is persistently missed, and that history does not exist.

\section{Hypertension Care and Digital Health in Sub-Saharan Africa}\label{sec:lr-regional}\label{sec:diagnosis}

\subsection{Burden, determinants and care gaps}

\Textcite{gafane2024} synthesise the hypertension literature of sub-Saharan Africa published between January 2017 and June 2023, drawn from MEDLINE, PubMed, CINAHL, African Journals Online and grey literature. Most countries of the region reported prevalence data over the period, confirming a high burden with poor detection and control. The authors relate the rise of hypertension to the shift towards a westernised lifestyle---salt, sugar and fat, physical inactivity, obesity, tobacco and alcohol---together with socio-economic and psychosocial factors, and they identify infection with the human immunodeficiency virus as a regional specificity; among the mechanisms still under investigation they list sodium handling, endothelial function and circadian profiles such as nocturnal dipping. They distinguish individual obstacles to care (health literacy, cost, distance) from system obstacles (medicine availability, staffing and training, uneven political will). Community-based task shifting improves linkage to care and medication adherence, but its effect on blood pressure over time is inconsistent.

Their inventory of national surveys is the most recent available for Central Africa (\cref{tab:central-africa}). Dispersion is wide, from 13.5\% to 42.1\%, and reflects the diversity of instruments as much as real differences; three countries of the sub-region have no data for the period. In Cameroon, the May Measurement Month campaign of 2019 found a prevalence of 20.8\%, with only 11.2\% of hypertensive participants controlled. The review is narrative rather than pooled, the surveys it gathers are heterogeneous, and some figures are inconsistent: the same pair of values (48\% and 34\%) is presented once as prevalence and once as diagnosis rate. It is used here for the regional profile and the care cascade, not for pooled prevalence.

\subsection{Why the episodic model of care fails}\label{sec:ltfu}\label{sec:inertia}

The WHO documents the mechanisms behind the failure of the cascade \parencite{who2023hypertension}. \emph{Loss to follow-up} often affects half of patients or more in resource-limited settings; the report therefore holds that screening is useful only if linked to effective follow-up, and it recommends opportunistic detection in health facilities together with the retention of identified patients rather than population screening. \emph{Therapeutic inertia}, the tendency not to intensify treatment despite uncontrolled blood pressure, explains why the last link of the cascade is the weakest; a simple standardised protocol helps reduce it. Lifestyle change alone lowers systolic pressure by only about 1.3~mmHg in primary-care trials, so almost every diagnosed patient needs medication, and its timely adjustment. \emph{Consultation time} is often below five minutes in primary care, which requires digital tools to be fast and to replace paper rather than add to it. Finally, \emph{paper records} are duplicated, lost and unavailable during the visit, whereas a secure longitudinal record is described as essential for hypertension, diabetes and other long-term conditions.

The core indicators of the HEARTS programme define what such a record must produce (\cref{tab:hearts}). Two of the four are temporal and can only be computed from a dated history of each patient, which a register of consultations does not keep.

The programmes cited by the WHO point in the same direction. In Sri Lanka, 86\% of enrolled patients return every three months and 57\% are controlled; in the Philippines, India and Cuba, part of the follow-up was moved out of the facility, towards the home or the community. These are associations observed in programmes that changed several components at once, not causal estimates. The Indian programme also shows that retention depends on medicine supply: stock-outs soon after launch discouraged patients from returning, so that a telemonitoring system deployed during stock-outs would probably not improve control, whatever its technical performance.

\subsection{Telemedicine evidence from Cameroon}\label{sec:telemedcam}\label{sec:bediang}\label{sec:cameroon-joint}

Two controlled evaluations of cardiovascular telemedicine have been conducted in Cameroonian primary care (\cref{tab:cameroon-pair}).

\paragraph{TELEMED-CAM (2008).} \Textcite{kingue2013} evaluated a hypertension management package in thirty rural health centres supported by a telemedicine centre at Yaoundé General Hospital. The package combined a standardised protocol restricted to generic drugs, lifestyle counselling, training of general practitioners and nurses, and a telecare channel through which providers reported patient data daily by text or voice message and received telephone feedback. Among 268 participants followed for 24 weeks, attendance at scheduled visits was higher in the intervention arm at every grade of hypertension ($p \leq 0.04$), and systolic pressure fell from the second visit. The benefit on control was confined to stage~III hypertension (no improvement in 16.7\% against 33.3\%, $p = 0.04$); for stages I and II the proportions at target were similar (65.2\% against 70\%, $p = 0.20$). Allocation was not strictly random, clustering was ignored, the arms differed at baseline (systolic pressure 169 against 161~mmHg, diabetes 9.1\% against 43.6\%), and telecare was only one component of the package, so its own effect cannot be isolated. The study also quantifies the cost of systematic remote review: about six calls per provider per day, of about five minutes each.

\paragraph{Tele-expertise in district hospitals (2016--2017).} \Textcite{bediang2021} compared two district hospitals equipped with a twelve-lead electrocardiograph and an internet tele-expertise platform (Mbouda and Akonolinga) with two matched control hospitals (Foumbot and Sa'a). Among 171 patients, access to an electrocardiogram with a cardiologist's opinion reached 98.9\% against 33.3\% (relative risk 2.97), the expert modified the local provider's diagnosis in 46.2\% of cases against 17.9\%, and patient satisfaction was higher. The study was not randomised and the groups differed at baseline; its primary outcome measures the availability of the service rather than its clinical effect. The reported blood pressure benefit (40.9\% against 17.9\% of patients with a favourable evolution) counts the 52 control patients who never returned as not improved; restricted to the patients actually reviewed at three months, the proportions become 41.3\% against 53.8\%. The specialist's opinion also took 10.3 days on average against a protocol maximum of 72 hours, no faster than in the control centres (11.2 days, $p = 0.82$): the bottleneck was expert availability, not transmission.

\subsection{Digital health landscape and barriers to adoption}\label{sec:digital-barriers}\label{sec:regional-landscape}

\Textcite{dodoo2022} reviewed 66 empirical studies and government reports on telemedicine in sub-Saharan Africa, drawn from eight databases under adapted PRISMA guidelines. Progress is markedly uneven between countries, and the barriers fall into six dimensions (\cref{tab:barriers}). Two findings bear directly on design: most projects lost momentum once external funding ended, and health professionals facing a heavy workload perceive telemedicine as an additional task. The authors also report that store-and-forward services suffered from low user satisfaction for lack of real-time interaction. Their inventory lists for Cameroon telecardiology, wound-care and tele-education services, including the TELEMED-CAM project, and records 41 countries with national digital health strategies. Francophone countries, Cameroon included, are under-represented in the literature they review.

\Textcite{agbeyangi2025} extend this picture with a PRISMA-guided systematic review of 53 studies published between 2014 and 2024, comparing South Africa, Kenya and Nigeria. South Africa leads adoption, including specialist teleconsultation and chronic disease management; Kenya shows strong integration of mobile health; Nigeria is advancing remote diagnosis under infrastructural constraints. Regulatory gaps, digital literacy and infrastructure remain the persistent barriers. Central Africa is absent from the comparison.

\Textcite{segun2025} assess the digital health landscape of the region from the perspective of cardiovascular care. More than 80\% of the population holds a mobile telephone subscription, mobile internet penetration was a quarter in 2023, and the adoption rate of smartphones is expected to reach 88\% by 2030; yet about 550 million people lack reliable access to electricity and the internet, and up to 18\% of countries had no registered cardiologist in 2020. The collapse of the Rwandan platform Babyl in 2023, after about 2.5 million users had registered, illustrates that adoption is not sustainability. Artificial intelligence remains largely untested in the region, with few validation studies in African populations and a documented risk that externally trained algorithms perform poorly there. As a perspective article without a declared search strategy, this source is used for orders of magnitude, not as pooled evidence.

Taken together, these sources describe a population that is reachable by mobile networks and a specialist who is not. A system that forwards every reading to a clinician reproduces the scarcity it aims to relieve; a system that sorts readings and orders the clinician's attention addresses it. They also impose offline operation, a simple interface for both patients and providers, and a documented operating cost.

\section{Mobile Health and Clinical Evidence}\label{sec:outpatient}\label{sec:evidence}

\subsection{What self-management applications offer}\label{sec:apps}

\Textcite{alessa2019} analysed the 186 hypertension self-management applications available in English on the two main application stores in January 2018, coding thirty of them in depth. Most offered a narrow set of functions: 58.1\% offered a single function, only 17.7\% three or more, and none all eight identified. Self-monitoring was present in 53.2\% of applications, feedback in 28.0\% and reminders in 21.0\%; most of the applications that tracked blood pressure required the patient to type in the value displayed by the monitor. None of the thirty analysed in depth had been scientifically evaluated or certified as a medical device, and none reported the involvement of patients in its design. Three applications claimed to measure blood pressure with the smartphone camera without any evidence of validity. Of the thirty, only twenty had a privacy policy, and fourteen of these declared sharing data with third parties. Coded with the taxonomy of behaviour change techniques, the applications contained a median of nine techniques, with self-monitoring, prompts and cues, and action planning present in all thirty. As a content analysis of a market snapshot, the study describes what applications contain, not whether they work.

\subsection{Pooled clinical effects}\label{sec:efficacy}

\Textcite{mao2020} pooled 51 randomised trials published between 2008 and 2019, with 13\,054 participants with diabetes or hypertension. Mobile health interventions reduced glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) by 0.39 points ($-0.50$ to $-0.29$), fasting glucose by 0.52~mmol/L, systolic pressure by 2.99~mmHg ($-4.19$ to $-1.80$) and diastolic pressure by 1.14~mmHg. Two subgroup results are decisive for this work. Interventions combining mobile health with professional management reduced systolic pressure by 6.17~mmHg ($-8.83$ to $-3.50$), whereas mobile health alone produced a non-significant 2.16~mmHg. And the effect on systolic pressure was present in developed countries ($-5.72$~mmHg) but absent in developing countries ($+0.25$~mmHg, $-3.10$ to $3.59$), whose trials used mobile health without professional management; the glycaemic effect, by contrast, was larger in developing countries ($-0.52$ against $-0.35$ points). Only one of the 51 trials was conducted in sub-Saharan Africa. Heterogeneity was moderate to high ($I^2$ of 57--67\%) and most trials were short.

\Textcite{zhou2024} focused on self-management through mobile applications for adults with hypertension: twenty randomised trials published from 2017 onwards, sixteen of them meta-analysed, with 4\,168 participants from nine countries, none in sub-Saharan Africa. Systolic pressure fell by 5.78~mmHg ($-7.97$ to $-3.59$; low certainty, $I^2 = 82\%$) and diastolic pressure by 3.28~mmHg (moderate certainty). The subgroup analyses link the effect to the design of the intervention (\cref{tab:subgroups}): interventions with at least eleven behaviour change techniques and those addressing several risk factors were markedly more effective, and three-month interventions tended to outperform longer ones, a difference that is not statistically significant ($P = .09$) and that the authors attribute to the weakening of habit after about twelve weeks. Fifteen of the twenty trials were at high risk of bias, and interventions based mainly on text messages or calls---the type most often evaluated in Africa---were excluded.

\Textcite{bandeira2024} updated the synthesis of randomised trials of mobile health interventions on blood pressure. They confirm statistically and clinically significant reductions across diverse populations and settings, and identify telemonitoring with clinician feedback as the most effective modality. Heterogeneity between interventions, short follow-up in several trials and the scarcity of data from low- and middle-income countries limit the generalisation of these results.

\subsection{A randomised trial in the target population}\label{sec:mihevc}

\Textcite{mihevc2025} conducted the trial most directly relevant to this work: a multicentre, open-label randomised trial in three Slovenian primary-care centres between 2021 and 2023, enrolling 128 patients aged 65 or more with both hypertension and type~2 diabetes. In addition to usual care, the intervention group measured blood pressure and glucose at home with Bluetooth devices that transmitted automatically through a smartphone to a cloud platform, and a telemedicine centre coordinated by a general practitioner reviewed the values according to a three-level protocol (green, yellow, red), adopted in this work (\cref{sec:triage}). After twelve months, systolic pressure was 6.9~mmHg lower than in the control group ($-11$ to $-2.7$), after a difference of 10.5~mmHg at six months, and HbA1c was 0.5 points lower.

Three further results matter. Telemonitoring had no effect on other cardiovascular risk factors, on diabetes-related quality of life or on lifestyle: the authors credit the clinical effect to better contact with the care team, better adherence, earlier recognition of worsening and treatment adapted on reliable measurements, not to behaviour change. Seven of the 64 patients of the intervention arm (10.9\%) withdrew in the first month because they could not use the equipment, despite one hour of individual training. And about a third of the systolic effect was lost between six and twelve months. The trial was conducted in a high-income setting, among older adults, without blinding; its application to low-resource settings requires adaptation.

Four conclusions emerge. First, mobile health and telemonitoring produce a modest but clinically relevant effect, of the order of $-3$ to $-7$~mmHg on systolic pressure and $-0.4$ to $-0.5$ points on HbA1c; these are intermediate outcomes, and none of the sources measures morbidity or mortality. Second, the effect depends on professional oversight: \textcite{mao2020}, \textcite{bandeira2024} and \textcite{mihevc2025} converge on this point by independent routes, and so do the Cameroonian studies of \cref{sec:telemedcam}. Third, the evidence from sub-Saharan Africa is almost absent, and the absence of effect observed in developing countries is plausibly a design gap---no clinician at the end of the chain---rather than a context effect. Fourth, two problems remain open: the erosion of the effect over time, and the inability of a fraction of patients to use the equipment. Telemonitoring must therefore be built as decision support linked to a clinician, not as a self-tracking tool handed to the patient.

\section{Devices, Sensors and Architectures}\label{sec:lr-iot}

\subsection{The normative position on measuring devices}\label{sec:metrology}

The WHO sets the frame within which any sensor must be assessed \parencite{who2023hypertension}. Manual devices should be replaced by independently validated automated electronic devices, and automated upper-arm cuffs are recommended. Cuffless devices, including popular connected wristbands, are not recommended for the management of hypertension, because of unresolved questions about their accuracy and their ability to track blood pressure change; as of 2023, no alternative to the validated upper-arm cuff is accurate enough for diagnosis and management. Only about one automated device in five on the market has evidence of validation.

\subsection{Non-invasive sensing of glucose and blood pressure}\label{sec:cuffless}

\Textcite{alhaddad2022} reviewed the non-invasive sensing modalities proposed for blood glucose, namely electrocardiography, electromagnetic sensing, bioimpedance and photoplethysmography (PPG), and the machine learning models applied to them. PPG- and ECG-based sensing combined with deep learning shows promise for detecting glucose trends and hypoglycaemia, but not for clinical-grade absolute measurement: many studies have small samples, inter-individual variation limits generalisation, and accuracy remains below the standards recognised by the Food and Drug Administration.

\Textcite{liu2025ppg} estimated blood pressure from the PPG signal recorded by a smartphone camera alone, without additional hardware. Combining waveform feature engineering with machine learning, and using SHAP values to trace each prediction back to interpretable physiological features, they report a strong correlation with reference devices and assess agreement by Bland--Altman analysis on 127 participants. The sample is modest and single-site, accuracy remains below that of clinical cuff devices, and generalisation across skin tones, devices and lighting conditions is not established.

These two studies, set against the WHO position and the unvalidated camera applications found by \textcite{alessa2019}, define a consistent position. Glucose must come from a glucometer and blood pressure from a validated upper-arm cuff, because these are the measurements on which clinical decisions rest. Smartphone PPG is a plausible low-cost source of heart rate and an exploratory source of blood pressure trends, to be evaluated rather than relied upon.

\subsection{IoT architectures for remote monitoring}

\Textcite{tai2024} address the difficulty of applying conventional machine learning to noisy, heterogeneous and high-dimensional IoT health data. They propose a human-centric deep learning technique for remote chronic disease monitoring, embedded in a standardised three-phase architecture: data acquisition, data processing and data visualisation. The architecture supports accurate and interpretable predictions in their evaluation, which was conducted in controlled conditions and does not address the connectivity constraints of low-resource settings.

\Textcite{efendi2025} describe and validate a complete IoT system for monitoring elderly people. Its three layers are a physical layer (an optical sensor for heart rate and oxygen saturation and a temperature sensor, read by an ESP32 microcontroller), a network layer (MQTT transmission to a Firebase real-time database) and an application layer (mobile application and website). Firebase was chosen for real-time synchronisation, built-in authentication, and local persistence with automatic merging when connectivity returns. Against a digital oximeter and thermometer, six participants measured for ten minutes at rest gave a mean absolute percentage error of 0.90\%, and XGBoost was the best of five classifiers of the user's state as stable or not stable. The system measures heart rate, oxygen saturation and temperature, not blood pressure or glucose; its validation sample is small, the reference instruments are not identified as validated, and its classifier is examined in \cref{sec:lr-ml}.

\Textcite{liu2025review} survey the use of IoT mobile sensing and machine learning for cardiovascular disease, cancer, chronic respiratory disease and diabetes. They classify devices into portable, wearable, intelligent clinical, remote, implantable and ingestible categories, describe a seven-layer platform from device access to decision support, and identify the weaknesses of traditional monitoring: manual processes, information silos, lack of real-time feedback and of early-warning mechanisms. They also note that electronic medical records require cleaning of duplicates, unit errors and values carried over identically between visits. Although presented as a systematic review, the article reports no protocol, flow diagram or number of included studies, so it is treated here as a narrative survey. Controlled settings dominate the studies it covers, and deployment in low- and middle-income countries is poorly represented.

Two architecture references complete the picture. \Textcite{mucchi2021} decompose remote monitoring into a body layer of worn sensors, an environment layer of ambient sensors, an edge layer, typically a smartphone, that checks the readings and raises a first warning, and a cloud layer that stores the data, performs long-term analysis and manages alerts; they tested their system with ten nurses on simulated patients for twenty days. Usability was rated well, but half of the nurses rated the system's efficiency for chronic disease as sufficient or below, two thirds rated their own digital skills as only fair, and they asked for faster alert notification and immediate feedback from the doctor. \Textcite{yazdani2018} add a clinician view that lists patients by priority rather than chronologically and a checkup period configurable for each patient, without implementing them.

The architectures converge on a common structure: acquisition, transmission and storage, processing, and presentation (\cref{tab:arch-status}). They disagree on the edge, and the disagreement matters: without an edge, every reading travels to the cloud and every decision is taken there; with an edge, readings can be checked and given a first warning locally, without a round trip. Where some 550 million people lack reliable electricity and internet access \parencite{segun2025}, the edge is a precondition rather than an optimisation. None of these architectures has been validated on patients in routine care in a low-resource setting, and none acquires blood pressure and glucose through its own sensors.

\section{Machine Learning for Clinical Risk Prediction}\label{sec:lr-ml}

\Textcite{chatrati2022} proposed a smart home monitoring system that analyses home blood pressure and glucose readings and notifies the healthcare provider when an abnormality is detected, combining conditional rules for hypertension with a learned classifier for diabetes. It is the closest published precedent of a system combining both measurements with machine learning. As described, however, it classifies single readings rather than series, and it has not been evaluated on patients in routine care.

\Textcite{liu2025review} report that artificial neural networks, support vector machines, random forests, gradient boosting and deep learning are the dominant algorithm families for chronic disease monitoring. The accuracies they collect cluster between 95\% and 100\%, whereas the only recurrent network applied to a genuine clinical prediction task, the readmission of lupus patients, reaches an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.70. They name the black-box nature of these models and their dependence on large labelled datasets as their main limitations.

The classifier comparison of \textcite{efendi2025} shows why such figures must be read with care. XGBoost reached an accuracy of 0.973 and the four other models exceeded 0.92. However, the label \emph{stable} is defined as all three parameters lying in their normal ranges, and each feature is recoded as low, normal or high using the same ranges, so the label is a deterministic function of the inputs:
\begin{equation}
  y = 1 \iff (x_1 = 1) \wedge (x_2 = 1) \wedge (x_3 = 1),
\end{equation}
and the perfectly balanced classes (2\,250 against 2\,250) show that the dataset was constructed rather than observed. The models recover a rule instead of predicting a state. Some reported metrics are also mutually inconsistent, and the training configuration described (epochs, sigmoid activation, Adam optimiser) does not apply to tree-based models. The result does not show that XGBoost outperforms other algorithms.

\Textcite{mendoza2025} provide the counter-example of a genuine prediction task. Using 1\,308 hypertensive adults from the nationally representative Mexican survey ENSANUT~2022, they predicted uncontrolled blood pressure (130/80~mmHg threshold, prevalence 30.3\%) from seven predictors selected by LASSO: sex, age, socio-economic status, years since diagnosis, history of diabetes, source of medical care and adherence to a nutritional plan. Random forest reached an AUC of 0.75 (0.72--0.77), logistic regression 0.61 and XGBoost 0.54 (0.49--0.60), the last being indistinguishable from chance. Variable importance and SHAP values ranked age and years since diagnosis first, and the data and code are public. The best model nevertheless detects only 51\% of uncontrolled cases (specificity 0.98); the logistic regression row is computed with a different positive class from the other two, and the discussion attributes to random forest a sensitivity that is in fact its specificity. The design is cross-sectional, and the predictors are demographic and administrative rather than physiological.

The same algorithm is thus the best in one study and the worst in the other. Reported performance is governed by how the task is constructed---the integrity of the label and the informativeness of the features---far more than by the algorithm; across clinical prediction studies, machine learning shows no systematic benefit over logistic regression \parencite{christodoulou2019}. Accuracy is moreover an improper measure when the positive class is rare: with prevalence $\pi$, a model that never alerts reaches $1-\pi$. Proper scoring rules \parencite{gneiting2007} and calibration \parencite{vancalster2019} are the measures this literature lacks. \Cref{tab:ml-studies} sets the studies side by side with the design adopted here.

Three lessons follow. Reported performance depends on the construction of the task before the algorithm. Ensemble tree models are plausible candidates, and interpretability through feature importance and SHAP is now expected practice, as \textcite{mendoza2025} and \textcite{liu2025ppg} both show. And no source forecasts a future physiological state from a patient's own series of blood pressure and glucose readings, nor evaluates such a model in a low-resource setting.

Tracking how a patient changes is a related problem. Patient-similarity research uses many distances without a common benchmark \parencite{parimbelli2018,sharafoddini2017}; the largest independent comparison of distance measures for series, nine measures on 38 data sets, separates lock-step measures, which compare aligned elements, from elastic ones \parencite{wang2013timeseries}; and statistical control charts, which compare a patient's readings with their own baseline, are promising in hypertension and diabetes but little evaluated \parencite{tennant2007}.

\section{Data Privacy and Ethics}\label{sec:lr-privacy}

\Textcite{alhammad2024} conducted the first systematic review of how patients view the protection of the data that mobile health applications collect about them---confidentiality, privacy and security: 33 studies published between 2014 and 2022, identified in PubMed, Ovid and ScienceDirect under PRISMA guidance. Patients' concerns about their data are a consistent and significant barrier to adoption. Encryption, secure authentication and regular risk assessment are recommended as baseline safeguards, and transparency about how data are handled improves trust. Most included studies come from high-income countries and measure self-reported perceptions rather than actual incidents.

These findings converge with earlier observations of this review: most commercial applications either lack a privacy policy or declare sharing data with third parties \parencite{alessa2019}, the legal and regulatory dimension is one of the six barriers to telemedicine in the region \parencite{dodoo2022}, and the reference implementation of \textcite{efendi2025} stores its data in a cloud region outside the country of use, which raises the question of data residency. Since the present work processes hospital-sourced patient data through a mobile and cloud pipeline, data protection is a design constraint from the start: anonymisation before modelling, encrypted transmission, role-based authentication, and identification of the applicable Cameroonian legal framework (\cref{sec:ethics-considerations}).

Three trends emerge from the chronology. Telemonitoring moves from self-management towards systems linked to a clinician, which the most recent evidence identifies as the condition of effectiveness \parencite{mao2020,bandeira2024,mihevc2025}. Prediction moves from rules towards machine learning, with interpretability tools such as SHAP becoming standard \parencite{mendoza2025,liu2025ppg}. And sensing moves towards cuffless and smartphone-based measurement \parencite{alhaddad2022,liu2025ppg}, with cloud platforms designed to work offline \parencite{efendi2025}.

Four points remain controversial. Cuffless blood pressure measurement is promising in research \parencite{liu2025ppg} but not accepted for clinical decisions \parencite{who2023hypertension}. The very high comorbidity reported for Central Africa rests on two small hospital series (\cref{sec:central-estimates}). Whether mobile health works in Africa is open, since the effect disappears in developing countries when no professional is involved \parencite{mao2020}. And the same algorithm, XGBoost, is reported as the best \parencite{efendi2025} and as the worst \parencite{mendoza2025}, a contradiction explained by how the tasks were constructed rather than by the algorithm (\cref{sec:task-not-algo}).

\section{Limits of the Existing Literature}\label{sec:lit-gaps}\label{sec:positioning}

The analysis reveals five limits that matter for the follow-up of patients with hypertension and diabetes in Central Africa.

\paragraph{Blind spots.} Central Africa is almost absent from the evidence: one of the 51 trials of \textcite{mao2020} was conducted in sub-Saharan Africa, the comparative reviews cover South Africa, Kenya and Nigeria \parencite{agbeyangi2025}, and francophone countries are under-represented \parencite{dodoo2022}. No evaluated system combines consultation records, home measurements and sensor data for patients with both hypertension and diabetes.

\paragraph{Methodological weaknesses.} The Cameroonian studies are not randomised and have unbalanced or incomplete groups \parencite{kingue2013,bediang2021}; most trials are short and measure intermediate outcomes only; the technical validations rely on few volunteers, favourable conditions and reference instruments of unknown validation \parencite{efendi2025}; and the machine learning studies suffer from label leakage, improper metrics or reporting errors, and are validated on the population they were trained on (\cref{sec:lr-ml}).

\paragraph{Absence of local data and of measured transfer.} No nationally representative estimate exists for Cameroon, and no artificial intelligence tool in circulation has been validated on an African population \parencite{segun2025}. Early-warning scores derived elsewhere lose discrimination in African wards and require local validation and recalibration \parencite{moore2017uva,tomas2026ews}; no learned model trained elsewhere has been validated on Cameroonian patients, so models trained elsewhere cannot be assumed valid here.

\paragraph{A rule without memory.} The only validated home protocol for both conditions \parencite{mihevc2025} uses fixed thresholds identical for all patients, reacts to the level of a reading rather than to its trend, and uses no learned model; no source forecasts a future state from a patient's own series of blood pressure and glucose readings.

\paragraph{Clinician time.} Systematic expert review does not scale \parencite{kingue2013,bediang2021}, yet no design sizes the alert volume or orders the clinician's work, and none reports its operating cost.

Four questions therefore remain to be explored: forecasting deterioration from home series rather than reacting to single readings; measuring whether such a forecast transfers to Cameroonian patients; detecting a change in the patient's state before a threshold is crossed; and ordering scarce clinician time, with the operating constraints of the system documented.

\section{Link with the Present Research}\label{sec:research-questions}\label{sec:link}

\paragraph{Continuity.} The present work builds directly on the existing studies. It adopts the validated three-level home protocol of \textcite{mihevc2025}, the layered architecture with an edge tier of \textcite{mucchi2021}, offline-first storage as in \textcite{efendi2025}, the priority-ordered clinician view of \textcite{yazdani2018}, and the modelling and interpretability practice of \textcite{mendoza2025}. It also follows the conclusion of the clinical literature that telemonitoring must be linked to a clinician.

\paragraph{Originality.} It differs from them on five points: a system designed to combine consultation records, home measurements and, possibly, sensor data for both conditions; a learned model applied to each patient's series, to forecast the level of the next reading rather than classify the current one; a hybrid design in which the guideline rule is a floor that the model can raise but never lower; models selected by a benchmark rather than chosen in advance, evaluated with proper scores against the guideline rule; and models developed on public multi-centre data and validated externally on Cameroonian patients, a transfer that no study identified in the region has measured.

The scientific achievements are clear. Care for both conditions fails at follow-up: in the African Region, 72\% of the hypertensive adults already diagnosed are not controlled. Telemonitoring lowers systolic pressure by 3 to 7~mmHg and HbA1c by about half a point when a clinician acts on the data; without one, no effect was shown in developing countries. Validated instruments, layered architectures and interpretable models are available. The gap is equally clear: the only validated protocol for both conditions triages single readings with fixed thresholds, reported machine learning performance depends on how the task is built---the same algorithm is the best in one study and no better than chance in another---and no learned model has been validated on Cameroonian patients. No system combining multi-source data with a forecast from each patient's series has been designed and evaluated for patients with hypertension and diabetes in Central Africa.

Filling this gap requires two things before any data are analysed: a formal statement of the problem, in which the label is sourced and cannot leak into the inputs (\cref{ch:framework}), and a methodology able to treat longitudinal data under local constraints and to evaluate predictive models without the biases identified in this chapter (\cref{ch:methodology}).
