\chapter{Methodology and System Implementation}\label{ch:methodology}\label{ch:data}\label{ch:architecture}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:meth-intro}

This chapter describes how the research was conducted and justifies each methodological choice. It instantiates the framework of \cref{ch:framework} to answer the four research questions: whether a telemonitoring system combining consultation records, home readings and risk prediction can be built under the constraints of Cameroonian care (RQ1), whether learned models forecast the level of the next reading better than the guideline rule (RQ2), at longer lead times (RQ3), and on Cameroonian patients (RQ4).

The literature imposes three requirements on the method (\cref{ch:review}): use longitudinal data whose instruments and quality can be assessed; evaluate predictive models without the biases found in previous work---label leakage, improper metrics, validation on the training population, overfitting of small samples; and respect an ethical and legal framework for real patient data. The chapter presents in turn the type of research, its setting and population, the data and how they were collected, the sampling plan, the treatment of the data, the features, the candidate models, the experimental protocol and the metrics; then the telemonitoring system built around them, the implementation details that make every result reproducible, the ethical considerations, the limitations of the methodology and a synthesis.

\section{Type and Approach of Research}\label{sec:research-type}\label{sec:analysis}

The research is \textbf{applied}: it addresses a practical problem, the follow-up of patients with chronic diseases, by designing an artefact, a telemonitoring system. It follows a design-science logic in three stages: requirements are derived from the literature (\cref{ch:review}), the system is designed and built, and its predictive component is evaluated on retrospective data. Within this frame, the evaluation of the predictive component is \textbf{experimental}, since models are compared under a protocol fixed in advance, and the analysis of the cohorts is \textbf{descriptive}. The approach is \textbf{quantitative} (\cref{tab:research-type}), since the hypotheses are measurable comparisons; the labels, however, are clinical judgements, so the partner physician is to validate the labelling rule and relabel a sample of readings. The data are secondary: no patient was recruited and no measurement was taken for this work. \Cref{fig:study-design} shows how the datasets enter the analysis: models are developed and compared on public multi-centre data only, and the Douala charts are used once, for external validation.

\section{Research Setting and Population}\label{sec:setting}

The research was carried out at the Laboratory of Computer Engineering, Data Science and Artificial Intelligence of the NHPSD and, for the Douala records, at the Laquintinie Hospital of Douala, a public hospital, under the supervision of an endocrinologist of the hospital (\cref{sec:ethics}). The deployment context is that described in \cref{sec:lr-regional}: patients followed episodically, paper records, few specialists and intermittent connectivity.

\subsection{Target population and eligibility}\label{sec:eligibility}

The target population of the system consists of adults with diagnosed hypertension, type~2 diabetes, or both, followed at a health facility and able to measure at home. The data used in this work come from two other populations, each with its own criteria. In eICU, a stay is eligible when the patient is an adult and hypertension or diabetes is recorded in the diagnoses or the past history; hypertensions of another nature (pulmonary, intracranial, portal, ocular, intra-abdominal, gestational) are excluded (\cref{app:data}). In Douala, all the observation charts made available by the hospital for the period are used; the admission diagnosis was not transcribed, so eligibility cannot be checked patient by patient. The criteria of a prospective collection with the system (\cref{tab:eligibility}) are adapted from the trial of \textcite{mihevc2025}; they would be finalised with the hospital and a research ethics committee before any recruitment.

\subsection{Harmonisation of the intensive-care records}\label{sec:harmonisation}

Intensive-care monitoring is far denser than a ward chart, so each stay is resampled to the rhythm of the charts: two slots a day, at 08:00 and 20:00, each taking the non-invasive cuff pressure, the pulse and the bedside glucose nearest to the slot within two hours (\cref{fig:harmonisation}). The slot hours are an assumption, since the charts record only morning and evening, and the tolerance is varied in a sensitivity analysis. Both datasets then share one sample definition, and the remaining differences---intensive care against the ward, the United States against Cameroon---are the shift that external validation measures.

\subsection{Validity and reliability}\label{sec:validity}\label{sec:instruments-obtained}

Validity and reliability are assessed at three levels. For the \textbf{instruments}, the devices of the Douala wards are not named, so their type and the consequences of their tolerance are inferred from the readings themselves (below); the eICU records come from routine monitoring devices whose validation is not reported either. For the \textbf{data}, controls applied at entry detect implausible, incoherent, duplicated and carried-forward values (\cref{sec:quality}). For the \textbf{labels}, a written and versioned rule fixes the level of every reading (\cref{sec:labelling}), and the partner physician is to relabel a random sample of readings without seeing the rule's output, with agreement measured by the quadratically weighted kappa, which suits ordered levels.

A blood pressure device is validated when it meets the universal standard ISO~81060-2 \parencite{stergiou2018,iso81060-2}: against a reference, the mean of the differences must not exceed 5~mmHg and their standard deviation 8~mmHg. A glucose meter meets ISO~15197:2013 when 95\% of its results lie within 15~mg/dL of the laboratory value below 100~mg/dL, and within 15\% above \parencite{iso15197}. Since the charts do not name the devices, two analyses of the readings bound what this uncertainty implies. \textbf{Terminal digits}: a manual aneroid or mercury scale is graduated in steps of 2~mmHg, so manual readings end in even digits with an excess of zeros, whereas an electronic display shows any integer \parencite{kottke2022}; a $\chi^2$ test of uniformity of the last digit tells the two apart. \textbf{Label uncertainty}: shifting every reading by the tolerance of its standard---5~mmHg for pressure, 15~mg/dL or 15\% for glucose---and counting the readings whose level changes measures how much of the disagreement between a model and the label is measurement noise that no model can remove. Two consequences follow: an error between adjacent levels must weigh less than an error across several levels (\cref{sec:metrics}), and the physician's relabelling measures how much of this noise reaches clinical judgement.

\section{Sampling Plan}\label{sec:sampling}\label{sec:capacity}

\paragraph{Sample size.} Whether the Douala charts suffice to develop a model is answered with the criteria of \textcite{riley2019partII,riley2020bmj}: the sample must keep the optimism of the model small (shrinkage $S \geq 0.9$), keep the apparent and adjusted $R^2$ within 0.05, and estimate the overall risk within $\pm 0.05$. The first criterion gives
\begin{equation}
  n \geq \frac{p}{(S - 1)\,\ln\bigl(1 - R^2_{\mathrm{CS}}/S\bigr)} ,
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the minimum number of samples, $p$ the number of candidate parameters of the model, $S$ the target shrinkage (0.9), and $R^2_{\mathrm{CS}}$ the anticipated Cox--Snell $R^2$, derived from an anticipated C statistic \parencite{riley2021cstat}. The criteria are computed with a port of the reference implementation \parencite{pmsampsize}, checked against its published example. The outcome is the alert of each condition. The samples of one patient are correlated, so the effective sample size divides their number by the design effect $1 + (\bar m - 1)\rho$ \parencite{kish1965}, with $\bar m$ the mean number of samples per patient and $\rho$ the intraclass correlation of the alert. Flexible learners need more still: random forests, support vector machines and neural networks may need several times as many events per variable as logistic regression to reach the same optimism \parencite{vanderploeg2014}.

\paragraph{Sampling method.} No sample was drawn: in Douala, all the charts made available by the hospital are used, a convenience sample of one site whose characteristics are reported so that its representativeness can be judged (\cref{sec:results-data}); in eICU, every eligible stay of the demonstration subset is used. eICU is preferred to the other public intensive-care databases, such as MIMIC-IV, because it covers many hospitals: the outer folds of the cross-validation can then be grouped by hospital, which estimates how a model transfers between sites before it meets the Douala patients \parencite{steyerberg2016}; a single-centre database cannot provide that estimate.

\paragraph{The role of each dataset.} The charts fall far below the criteria (\cref{sec:results-sample-size}). The candidate models are therefore developed and compared on eICU only, and the Douala charts serve for external validation, a transfer that no study identified in the region has measured for a learned model; early-warning scores derived elsewhere lose discrimination in African wards and require local validation and recalibration \parencite{moore2017uva,tomas2026ews}, and a Ghanaian study is validating intensive-care scores derived in high-income countries on West African patients \parencite{hayfronbenjamin2026}. With about 64 haemodynamic and 112 glycaemic alert targets, below the 100 events and 100 non-events recommended for external validation \parencite{collins2016,riley2021external}, the Douala estimates are reported with patient-level intervals and read as exploratory.

\paragraph{Model capacity.} The effective size of the Douala samples is small against the capacity of any flexible model, and above all of a recurrent one. The number of trainable parameters of an LSTM layer is
\begin{equation}
  P = 4\bigl[u(f+u) + u\bigr] ,
\end{equation}
where $u$ is the number of hidden units and $f$ the number of inputs at each time step; the factor 4 counts the three gates and the candidate cell of the LSTM, each with an input matrix ($u \times f$), a recurrent matrix ($u \times u$) and a bias vector ($u$). Even a small layer ($u = 16$, $f = 10$) has 1\,728 parameters, that is 24 to 33 per independent Douala sample. Trained on the charts, a recurrent model would fail for lack of data, and the comparison of H3 would measure the sample size rather than the models. Four responses were possible: increase the sampling frequency for a sub-cohort; compare the models on a public dataset and use the local cohort for external validation; restrict the comparison to a few simple models; or report a recurrent model as a negative result limited by capacity. The second was adopted: it gives the deep models a sample on which their comparison means something, and it turns the small local cohort into a test of transportability rather than a development set.

\subsection{Outliers: artefact or event?}\label{sec:outliers}\label{sec:mad-shrinkage}

In a risk-detection system the outlier is often the event of interest: a systolic reading of 185~mmHg is a statistical outlier for a patient whose median is 138, and also a red alert. An automatic cleaning step would delete the positive class. The dividing line is therefore physiological plausibility, not statistical rarity. A statistical threshold is used for flagging only, with the median absolute deviation (MAD), which is not inflated by the outliers it detects, shrunk towards the cohort value because a personal MAD from ten readings is unstable:
\begin{equation}
  z_{i,t} = \frac{x_{i,t} - \operatorname{median}(x_i)}{1.4826\,\widehat{\mathrm{MAD}}_i}, \qquad
  \widehat{\mathrm{MAD}}_i = w_i\,\mathrm{MAD}_i + (1-w_i)\,\mathrm{MAD}_{\text{pooled}}, \quad w_i = \frac{n_i}{n_i + n_0},
\end{equation}
where $x_{i,t}$ is the reading of patient $i$ at time $t$ on one channel; $\operatorname{median}(x_i)$ and $\mathrm{MAD}_i$ are the median and the median absolute deviation of the patient's readings on that channel; $\mathrm{MAD}_{\text{pooled}}$ is the cohort value towards which the personal value is shrunk; $w_i$ is the weight of the personal value, $n_i$ the patient's number of readings and $n_0$ the number at which the personal value receives half the weight; and the factor 1.4826 makes the MAD comparable with a standard deviation when the readings are normally distributed. With $n_0 = 20$, a patient with ten readings takes one third of the estimate from their own data; as the series grows, the estimate becomes personal. A reading is flagged when $\lvert z_{i,t}\rvert > 3.5$ \parencite{iglewicz1993}; flags serve clinician review and never enter the features.

\subsection{Missing data}\label{sec:missingness}\label{sec:masking}\label{sec:mask-models}

Imputation is valid when data are missing completely at random (MCAR) or at random given observed values (MAR), and invalid when missingness depends on the unobserved value itself (MNAR). Each source of this work has its own mechanism. Consultation data are MNAR, because attendance depends on the patient's state; home data are MNAR by a different route, because discouraging readings may be omitted; only sensor gaps (battery, connection) are approximately MCAR. In the wards, the vital signs follow the chart schedule, but glucose is ordered according to the clinical picture, so whether it is measured depends on the patient's state: its absence is MNAR as well.

The direction of the resulting bias matters. If abnormal values are less likely to be recorded, the observed values are biased towards normal, and mean imputation or forward filling teaches the model that absence predicts stability, whereas it predicts deterioration---a sign error that more data cannot correct, and one that contradicts the system's own rule that a silence must raise an event (\cref{sec:functions}). Absence is therefore represented as information: the number of glucose readings in the window and the time since the last one are features. Tree ensembles route missing values at each split. The recurrent model GRU-D \parencite{che2018} receives the mask of what was measured and decays the last value towards the training mean as the gap grows,
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  \tilde{x}^{(c)}_t = m^{(c)}_t x^{(c)}_t + \bigl(1 - m^{(c)}_t\bigr)\bigl[\gamma^{(c)}_t\, x^{(c)}_{\text{last}} + (1-\gamma^{(c)}_t)\,\bar{x}^{(c)}\bigr], \\
  \gamma^{(c)}_t = \exp\bigl(-\max\{0,\ \mathbf{w}_c^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_t + b_c\}\bigr),
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where $c$ is the channel and $t$ the time step; $x^{(c)}_t$ is the value standardised on the training data, $m^{(c)}_t$ the mask (1 if the channel is measured at step $t$, 0 otherwise) and $\tilde{x}^{(c)}_t$ the value passed to the network; $x^{(c)}_{\text{last}}$ is the last observed value of the channel and $\bar{x}^{(c)}$ its training mean; $\gamma^{(c)}_t$, between 0 and 1, is the decay factor; $\boldsymbol{\delta}_t$ is the vector of the times elapsed since the last observation of each channel; and $\mathbf{w}_c$ and $b_c$ are learned with the network. A long gap thus moves the input towards the training mean, and the hidden state of the network is decayed towards zero in the same way \parencite{che2018}. Each channel has its own decay, since a three-day-old pressure says more about today than a three-day-old glucose. The decay is always used together with the mask, since on its own it would again assert stability during long gaps. The proportional-odds model, which cannot accept missing inputs, receives the training median with a missingness indicator, which keeps the absence visible to the model.

The features keep their clinical units, since the label is defined on absolute thresholds: normalising each patient's values by their own median and dispersion would hide how far a reading lies from a threshold, which is precisely what the label measures, so the patient's own distribution is used only to flag extreme values (\cref{sec:outliers}). The linear and neural models are sensitive to the scale of their inputs, so each input is standardised with the mean and standard deviation of the training fold; the tree ensembles need no scaling (\cref{tab:model-prep}).

Four remarks explain what the table leaves out or why it includes a feature.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No signed margin to a threshold.} For a fixed threshold $\theta$, the margin $x_{\text{last}} - \theta$ is an affine function of the last value: a linear model absorbs it into its intercept, and a tree, which splits on order, finds exactly the same partitions. The margin adds a column and no information; the same holds for the grade band, a coarsening of the last value.
  \item \textbf{No disease duration or treatment.} Years since diagnosis and treatment are recorded neither in the charts nor in eICU, and a feature that exists in only one dataset cannot be validated externally.
  \item \textbf{Pulse pressure helps trees.} A tree splits on one variable at a time and would need many splits to approximate a condition on SBP $-$ DBP; supplying it explicitly turns that staircase into one split.
  \item \textbf{The glucose count is informative.} Glucose is ordered for some patients and not for others (\cref{sec:missingness}); the count of glucose readings lets the model use that pattern openly instead of through imputed values.
\end{itemize}

A window of $n$ readings cannot carry more than $n$ independent quantities. With two readings, the sample standard deviation is $\lvert x_2 - x_1\rvert/\sqrt{2}$, the mean absolute successive difference is $\lvert x_2 - x_1\rvert$ and the slope is $\pm\lvert x_2 - x_1\rvert/\Delta t$: level, dispersion, slope and last value are only two quantities, a level and a signed difference. The feature sets are therefore kept small and fixed before any result (\cref{tab:feature-sets}). The primary set spends its columns on the channels that carry the decision: systolic pressure and glucose get a level and a trend, diastolic pressure and pulse a level, and the glucose count summarises how the patient is monitored; dispersion is left out because a short window estimates it poorly.

The window length is a hyperparameter, searched over 24, 48, 72 and 96~hours, and what each length can carry follows from the number of readings it holds (\cref{tab:window-length}). Because the length is searched, the set of forecast targets must not depend on it, or each length would be evaluated on different targets: a reading is an anchor when the shortest window, 24~hours, holds at least two pressure readings, and longer windows add history to the same anchors. Overlapping windows multiply the rows but not the information, so the patient is the independent unit: all the windows of a patient fall on the same side of every split, and intervals are computed at the patient level.

\section{Experimental Protocol}\label{sec:evaluation}\label{sec:training}\label{sec:cv-structure}\label{sec:repeats}

All constraints combine into a nested cross-validation on the development data (\cref{fig:nested-cv}). Five outer folds estimate performance and are grouped by hospital, so that every test fold contains hospitals the model has never seen; four inner folds, grouped by patient, select the window and the hyperparameters. The search uses the tree-structured Parzen estimator of Optuna \parencite{bergstra2011,akiba2019} over a space declared before any run, split into structural hyperparameters (window, depth, units, components) and training hyperparameters (learning rate, penalties, dropout, class weights); each configuration is scored by its ranked probability score on pooled inner-validation predictions, and the configuration kept is the one with the lowest selection cost (\cref{sec:metrics}). The number of configurations per candidate is fixed in advance (\cref{app:config}), since the optimism of a selection grows with it \parencite{cawley2010}. Across families, the lowest inner selection cost gives the \emph{selected} model of the fold, and its outer result estimates the whole procedure. The procedure is repeated three times with different fold assignments, and two models are compared with the corrected resampled $t$ test, which accounts for the overlap between training sets \parencite{nadeau2003}. \Cref{fig:search} details what happens inside one outer fold.

Each selected configuration is then refitted on all the development data and applied once to the Douala charts; nothing is tuned on them, and their intervals come from a bootstrap that resamples patients \parencite{collins2016}. The prevalence of the alert differs between intensive care and Douala wards, which shifts calibration even for a model that transfers well, so performance is reported as is, and again after updating the intercept alone on the Douala data \parencite{janssen2008}: the first is the test of transportability (H4), the second shows what a local recalibration recovers. Fitted and scored on the same patients, the update would be judged on the outcomes it was fitted to; its Brier score and net benefit are therefore computed with each patient's intercept fitted on the other patients. Class imbalance is handled inside the training fold only, since oversampling before the split would place copies of the same window in training and test. Whether class weights help is a training hyperparameter of the search; a classifier trained with weights $w_k$ overstates each level in proportion to its weight, so its probabilities are corrected exactly by Bayes' rule before use, $p_k = (p'_k/w_k) / \sum_j p'_j/w_j$, where $p'_k$ is the raw output of the classifier for level $k$, $p_k$ the corrected probability, and the sum runs over the four levels. The correction is not cosmetic: in the two-class case with the conventional weight $w = (1-\pi)/\pi = 49$ at a prevalence $\pi = 0.02$, a raw output of 0.5 corresponds to a true probability of exactly 0.02, so a clinician shown 0.5 would read a risk twenty-five times too high. H2 is tested by repeating the benchmark with embargoes of 12 and 36~hours, for targets 24 and 48~hours ahead.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}\label{sec:metrics}

Accuracy decomposes as
\begin{equation}
  \text{Accuracy} = \pi\,\text{Sensitivity} + (1-\pi)\,\text{Specificity},
\end{equation}
where $\pi$ is the prevalence of the positive class, Sensitivity the share of positive cases predicted positive, and Specificity the share of negative cases predicted negative. When positives are rare, a model that never predicts them therefore reaches $1-\pi$. The logistic regression of \textcite{mendoza2025} illustrates this: its accuracy of 0.70 is close to the proportion of controlled patients, while its specificity is 0.03. The measures are therefore chosen by explicit criteria \parencite{vancalster2025}: a clear focus---discrimination, calibration, overall accuracy of the probabilities or clinical utility---and, for overall accuracy, a proper scoring rule \parencite{gneiting2007}. Accuracy, the F1 score and recall at a fixed threshold fail these criteria: they are improper, depend on an arbitrary cut-point and mix statistical with decision performance. \Cref{tab:metrics} lists the admissible measures; the positive class is the alert, and every measure carries a patient-level confidence interval. Performance is also reported by sex and by age band (below and above 65~years), and across the hospitals of the outer folds.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\caption{Admissible performance measures.}\label{tab:metrics}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.6cm} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.2cm} X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Focus} & \textbf{Measure} & \textbf{Why} \\
\midrule
Overall, four levels & ranked probability score (RPS) \parencite{epstein1969} & proper for ordered levels: an error across several levels costs more than an adjacent one \\
Overall, alert & Brier score, log loss & proper \\
Discrimination & area under the ROC curve (AUROC) of the alert & independent of any threshold \\
Calibration & intercept and slope \parencite{vancalster2019} & a probability shown to a clinician must mean what it says \\
Clinical utility & net benefit, decision curve \parencite{vickers2006} & true alerts weighed against false alarms at the clinical threshold \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

For $n$ targets with observed levels $y_j \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ and forecast probabilities $p_{j,k}$ of each level $k$, the overall and utility measures are
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  \mathrm{RPS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{k=0}^{K-2}\bigl(F_{j,k} - O_{j,k}\bigr)^2, \\
  \mathrm{Brier} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigl(\hat p_j - e_j\bigr)^2, \qquad
  \mathrm{NB}(p_t) = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{n} - \frac{\mathrm{FP}}{n}\,\frac{p_t}{1-p_t},
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where $K = 4$ is the number of levels; $F_{j,k} = \sum_{l \leq k} p_{j,l}$ is the forecast probability that target $j$ is at level $k$ or below, and $O_{j,k}$ equals 1 if $y_j \leq k$ and 0 otherwise; $\hat p_j = p_{j,2} + p_{j,3}$ is the alert probability and $e_j$ equals 1 if the target is an alert ($y_j \geq 2$) and 0 otherwise; $p_t$ is the decision threshold; and $\mathrm{TP}$ and $\mathrm{FP}$ are the numbers of true and false alerts when an alert is raised at $\hat p_j \geq p_t$. The RPS is 0 for a perfect forecast; forecasting \emph{stable} with certainty for a critical target costs 1, and forecasting \emph{high} costs 1/3, so an error across several levels costs more than an adjacent one. The calibration intercept $a$ and slope $b$ come from the logistic recalibration
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{logit} P(e_j = 1) = a + b \operatorname{logit}(\hat p_j),
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{logit}(p) = \ln\bigl(p/(1-p)\bigr)$; the slope is fitted freely and the intercept with $b$ fixed to 1, so that a well-calibrated model has $a = 0$ and $b = 1$ \parencite{vancalster2019}. A negative intercept means that the model overstates the risk on average, a positive one that it understates it; a slope below 1 means that its probabilities are too extreme.

In the inner folds, configurations are compared on one number, the \textbf{selection cost}. Four terms---the RPS, the AUROC, the calibration error $\lvert a\rvert + \lvert \ln b\rvert$ for intercept $a$ and slope $b$, and the net benefit averaged over thresholds of 0.1 to 0.3---are standardised across the configurations compared (minus the median, divided by the scaled MAD, oriented so that lower is better) and combined:
\begin{equation}
  C = \sum_m w_m\, z_m, \qquad \textstyle\sum_m w_m = 1 ,
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the selection cost of a configuration, $m$ runs over the four terms, $z_m$ is the standardised value of term $m$ and $w_m$ its weight. The weights express how much each focus matters clinically, so they are a value judgement, not a parameter: tuning them on the data that judge the models would let any weighting favour a chosen model. They are equal in the provisional analysis, to be replaced by weights derived from the costs of errors stated by the partner physician, and a sensitivity analysis draws them at random to report how often the selected configuration remains first. The outer folds report every measure separately, never the composite alone. 

Interpretability follows two lessons of the literature. An explanation describes a model, not a phenomenon: explaining a model that does not discriminate, such as an XGBoost at an AUC of 0.54 \parencite{mendoza2025}, has little value, so interpretability is reported only for a model shown to beat persistence. And importance measures are unreliable when features are correlated: permuting one of two correlated features lets the model fall back on the other, and gain-based importance credits whichever happens to be chosen at a split \parencite{strobl2008,gregorutti2017}. Systolic and diastolic pressures are strongly correlated, so the features of one channel are permuted together, and the loss in RPS measures what the channel contributes (grouped permutation importance); for CatBoost, exact tree SHAP values are reported in addition \parencite{lundberg2017}, and the direction of their effects is checked against physiology.

The measures of state evolution (\cref{tab:state-measures}) are compared on the same samples: a measure is useful if its distance ranks higher the samples in which the level of the next reading differs from that of the baseline state (primary outcome), or becomes an alert (secondary outcome). Each is evaluated by its AUROC with a patient-level interval, its scales are estimated on eICU only, and the measure retained is the best on the primary outcome, or the simplest candidate whose interval contains the best value.

\subsection{Architecture}\label{sec:layers}

The system has four parts (\cref{fig:architecture}). A cross-platform \textbf{application} (Flutter) serves the patient and the clinician. A managed document database (\textbf{Cloud Firestore}) holds the record and synchronises the applications, with a local cache that turns a reading entered offline into a delayed one. A local \textbf{risk service} (FastAPI) scores a patient's recent readings with the model selected by the benchmark, using the same code as the research pipeline, so that the features seen in deployment cannot drift from those seen in training. And a local \textbf{worker} runs the server functions: measurement check, escalation, notification, the non-transmission sweep and the escalation of events left unreviewed. Hosting the service and the worker online is outside the scope of this work; without them, the application still applies the home triage on the phone.

\subsection{Data model and rules}\label{sec:record-schema}\label{sec:rules}

Each patient's readings, events and messages are subcollections of their profile, so that one security rule governs them and the readings are read in time order with one query. A reading holds one measurement session: the measurement time stated by the patient and the arrival time set by the server, the retained values, the first reading of a protocol pair kept for audit, the context of a glucose reading, and the quality flag set by the worker. The record is append-only: the rules refuse any change or deletion of a reading, and a correction is a new reading that names the one it supersedes. Each write is validated on the server (known fields, plausible ranges, coherent pressure pair, measurement time at most thirty days before arrival). An event stores what raised it (rule, model, verification profile, or a silence longer than the patient's checkup period), the triggering values, the threshold and model versions, and the clinician's verdict and action; it is closed once, and its identifier is derived from what raised it, so the same alert raised by the phone and by the worker is one document (\cref{fig:data-model}).

\subsection{Interfaces}\label{sec:patient-app}\label{sec:dashboard}

The \textbf{patient} enters the two pressure readings of the protocol, of which the second is retained, and may date a reading taken earlier; the glucose unit is fixed to mmol/L, a value outside its plausible range is refused with a message naming the unit, and the measurement context is required for glucose because the target depends on it. A reading at the red level brings up a local warning at once, whatever the connectivity. The home screen shows the level of the latest readings with the one instruction the patient may receive---contact the health centre, or seek care today---never a dose and never a probability; the verification profile to carry out and its progress; the fourteen-day trend with the target band; the evolution of each channel; and the outcome of the clinician's reviews. The \textbf{clinician} works from a queue of unreviewed events, red before yellow and then oldest first, each row showing the source of the alert, the triggering value, the fourteen-day trend and the versions, and closes an event with a verdict (true alert, false alert, uncertain) and the action taken in two or three taps; the verdicts are the clinically adjudicated labels that escape the circularity of rule-derived labels. \Cref{app:screens} shows the screens.

Two display rules apply on the clinician's side. A raw score from a weighted model is never displayed, since an output of 0.5 may correspond to a true probability of 0.02 (\cref{sec:evaluation}); only the corrected value is shown, and the tier with its reason often communicates more honestly than any decimal. And the trigger source is always shown: a rule trigger is a guideline threshold crossing, whereas a model trigger, on samples of this size, is weaker evidence, and showing both the same way would make the system distrusted after its first false alarm. The dashboard must also replace a task rather than add one, since health professionals in the region see telemedicine as additional work \parencite{dodoo2022}: the verdict both closes the alert and serves as the clinical note.

\subsection{The health assistant}\label{sec:assistant}

The patient application contains a conversational assistant that answers questions in French or English. It relies on a general-purpose language model reached through the Firebase platform and receives, at the start of each conversation, an instruction that describes its role, the home targets of \cref{tab:thresholds} and the patient's context: age, sex, recorded conditions, prescribed medications and the readings of the last four days. No identifier is sent: neither name, e-mail nor telephone number. The assistant explains what a reading means and why follow-up matters, gives general information on diet, salt, physical activity and adherence to the treatment already prescribed, and helps the patient judge whether a situation can wait for the next appointment. Four rules, stated as overriding every other instruction, keep it within the boundary of automation (\cref{tab:assistant-guardrails}); it cannot write a reading, change a profile or alter a risk level.

\section{Ethical Considerations}\label{sec:ethics-considerations}\label{sec:ethics}\label{sec:legal}

The research involves real patients and their health data. Three principles apply: informed consent where patients are involved directly, anonymity and confidentiality of the data, and compliance with the applicable ethical and legal standards.

The eICU data are de-identified and distributed by PhysioNet under its terms \parencite{pollard2018,goldberger2000}. The Douala charts are a secondary use of routine records, transcribed without names under the research authorisation of the Laquintinie Hospital of Douala (No.~185/\allowbreak AR/\allowbreak MINSANTE/\allowbreak DHL/\allowbreak CM of 25 September 2026, for the period from 28 September to 4 November 2026). The authorisation places the work under the supervision of an endocrinologist of the hospital and under the hospital's code of ethics and professional conduct, and requires that any publication preserve the interests of the hospital and of its staff and that a copy be deposited at its documentation centre. A prospective study with home measurements, which involves patients directly, would in addition require the opinion of a research ethics committee. Removing names is not enough to anonymise a small cohort: before any analysis file is shared, ages are grouped in five-year bands and calendar dates replaced by the time elapsed since each patient's first reading, which is all the analysis uses. Personal data in Cameroon are governed by Law No.~2024/017 on the protection of personal data \parencite{cameroon2024data}, which makes any transfer abroad conditional on the authorisation of a supervisory authority, and the security of electronic communications by Law No.~2010/012 \parencite{cameroon2010cyber}; the region of the database is therefore chosen before the first write, since it is fixed at creation and cannot be changed without migrating the data. The health assistant sends conditions, medications and readings to a provider outside Cameroon, which requires the patient's informed consent.

The trial of \textcite{mihevc2025} complied with the European General Data Protection Regulation because it took place in the European Union. A system operated in Cameroon for Cameroonian patients falls outside its scope, and claiming compliance with it would hide the need to identify the law that does apply. Its principles---lawfulness, purpose limitation, data minimisation, storage limitation, integrity and confidentiality---are adopted here as a voluntary standard, while the applicable framework is Cameroonian. Under a prospective collection, the consent document would cover eight points: what is collected; who sees it; where it is stored and under which law; how long it is kept; that participation does not replace clinical care; that withdrawal is possible; what happens to collected data on withdrawal; and who keeps the link between pseudonym and identity. The legal framework thus determines the storage region and the consent document, which must both precede any collection.

\section{Limitations of the Methodology}\label{sec:meth-limits}

The following limitations were known before the results; for each, the expected effect on the results is stated, and \cref{sec:limitations} returns to them with the results in hand.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Small local sample, foreign development data.} The 32 Douala patients are far below the size needed to develop a model, so the models are developed on United States intensive-care data and only validated locally. Intensive-care patients are monitored and treated differently from ward patients, so performance in Douala is expected to be lower than in development; that gap is a result, measured with wide intervals.
  \item \textbf{Demonstration subset.} The open subset of eICU is about eighty times smaller than the full database, which is expected to limit above all the glycaemic task, where glucose is rarely measured in intensive care.
  \item \textbf{Inpatients, not home users.} Both datasets come from hospitalised patients, sicker than the outpatients a telemonitoring system serves, so high and critical levels are more frequent than at home, and no model can be transferred to home data without revalidation.
  \item \textbf{Data missing not at random.} Glucose is ordered for some patients and not for others, so its absence carries information. One label per condition prevents a missing value from being read as a normal one, but the features still see the pattern of measurement, which differs between the two settings.
  \item \textbf{Labels derived from thresholds.} The primary labels come from guideline rules, so a model may partly learn the rule; the physician's relabelling, immune to this, is planned on a sample only, and no clinician verdict exists in retrospective data.
  \item \textbf{Unknown instruments and hand transcription.} The devices are not named and every value was copied by hand from paper. The controls catch impossible values, not plausible errors, and the label of readings near a threshold is uncertain.
  \item \textbf{Diagnoses and hours not recorded.} The admission diagnosis was not transcribed, so the Douala cohort may include patients with neither condition; and the charts record morning and evening, not the hour, so the eICU slots at 08:00 and 20:00 and their tolerance are assumptions, tested by a sensitivity analysis.
  \item \textbf{Provisional clinical choices.} The weights of the selection cost and the tier cut-points await the costs of errors stated by the partner physician; a sensitivity analysis on the weights measures how much the selected model depends on them.
  \item \textbf{No clinical outcome.} A hospital stay of a few days cannot measure outcomes such as cardiovascular events; the evaluation concerns prediction and the system, not clinical effectiveness.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Synthesis and Transition}\label{sec:meth-synthesis}

\Cref{tab:meth-summary} summarises the methodological choices. Together they answer the research questions while avoiding the weaknesses identified in the literature: instruments whose type and tolerance are assessed from the data, a forecasting task instead of label recovery, proper measures and a baseline from the guideline rule, a model selected by a protocol fixed before the data were seen, and an external validation on Cameroonian patients. Two principles run through the chapter. Quality controls flag rather than delete, because an outlier may be the event the system must detect, and the absence of a measurement is treated as information. And every step that learns from the data is fitted inside the training fold, so that the outer estimates and the Douala validation are honest.
